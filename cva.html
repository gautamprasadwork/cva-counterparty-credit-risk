Pricing counterparty risk

A bank's derivative contracts are worth less if the institution on the other side might fail before paying. This project puts a number on that risk, then tests what actually reduces it.

Problem

Two banks sign a derivative contract. Each depends on the other staying solvent until it settles. If one fails first, the other loses whatever it was owed. The credit valuation adjustment is the price of that risk, and banking regulation requires it to be held as capital.

  • A derivative has no fixed balance owed. Its value moves every day with the market.
  • It can be worth money to either side, so the amount at risk keeps changing.
  • Exposure at the moment of default therefore has to be simulated, not looked up.
Problem statement

Calculate the credit valuation adjustment on a portfolio of two derivative contracts held against one bank counterparty, and measure how far netting and collateral reduce it.

Solution

The charge is a sum across every month in the life of the portfolio. Three quantities are multiplied together in each month, and the total is scaled by the share of exposure that would actually be lost.

CVA = LGD × Σ [ exposure × default probability × discount factor ]
  • Exposure. How much would be owed at that date.
  • Default probability. The chance the counterparty fails during that month.
  • Discount factor. What a future loss is worth today.
  • Loss given default. 60 percent, the share not recovered in a failure.
Stage one

Simulate the market

  • Ten thousand possible futures for the exchange rate and the interest rate.
  • Each stepped forward one month at a time across three years.
  • The exchange rate follows geometric Brownian motion, which keeps it positive.
  • The interest rate follows arithmetic Brownian motion, which allows negative rates.
Stage two

Measure the exposure

  • Both contracts revalued along every one of the ten thousand futures.
  • Only positive values count, since a loss arises only when a contract is owed to us.
  • Averaging across all futures gives the expected exposure at each date.
  • The swap carries almost all of it, at roughly ten times the size of the forward.
Stage three

Price the default risk

  • Default probability was read from market prices rather than assumed.
  • Credit default swaps pay out if a company fails, so their quotes imply how likely that is.
  • Working through the quoted maturities one at a time recovers a survival curve.
  • That curve becomes a probability of default in each individual month.
Judgement call

Correlation was measured before it was modelled

The brief asked for the two risk factors to be simulated together, linked by their historical correlation. That correlation was measured first and came out at 0.014, close to zero.

Factors that barely move together gain nothing from being linked, so they were simulated separately and the reasoning recorded. Same answer, simpler model.

The swap drives the charge

It carries ten times the notional of the forward, and its exposure grows toward maturity instead of fading away.

Netting saved less than expected

The two risk factors barely move together and one contract is far larger, so there is little for the offsets to work against.

Collateral was the effective tool

Exchanging margin every month removed more than four fifths of the charge, leaving only the movement between calls.
